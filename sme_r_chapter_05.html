6.2 Theoretical Background

Understanding Diagnostic Tests: Precision, Agreement, and Accuracy

Before analyzing diagnostic test data, let’s understand what makes a good test. These concepts are fundamental to diagnostic epidemiology and are covered in standard texts (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003):

6.2.1 What Makes a Good Diagnostic Test?

Imagine you have a medical test that’s supposed to detect a disease. You want to know: Is this test any good? To answer this, we need to understand three key concepts:

6.2.2 1. Precision (Reliability)

Precision asks: “If I test the same sample multiple times, do I get the same result?” It’s like asking if a scale gives the same weight when you weigh something three times.

  • High precision: The test gives consistent results (like a reliable scale)
  • Low precision: The test gives different results each time (like a broken scale)

Why it matters: A test that’s not precise is unreliable - you can’t trust its results, even if it’s sometimes right.

6.2.3 2. Agreement (Inter-rater Reliability)

Agreement asks: “Do different people (or different tests) agree when measuring the same thing?” It’s like asking if two doctors examining the same patient would give the same diagnosis.

  • High agreement: Different observers get similar results
  • Low agreement: Different observers disagree

Why it matters: If different people can’t agree on the results, the test isn’t useful in practice.

6.2.4 3. Accuracy (Validity)

Accuracy asks: “Does the test correctly identify who has the disease and who doesn’t?” This is about being right, not just consistent.

Key measures: - Sensitivity: Can the test find people who really have the disease? (True positive rate) - Specificity: Can the test correctly identify people who don’t have the disease? (True negative rate) - ROC Curve: A graph showing how sensitivity and specificity trade off at different cutoffs

6.2.5 Understanding These Concepts

Think of it like a security system: - Precision: Does the alarm go off consistently when you test it? - Agreement: Do different security guards agree when the alarm goes off? - Accuracy: Does the alarm go off for real threats and not for false alarms?

6.2.6 Why All Three Matter

A test can be precise (consistent) but inaccurate (wrong). Or it can be accurate on average but imprecise (sometimes right, sometimes wrong). For a test to be truly useful, it needs to be precise, show good agreement, AND be accurate.

In simple terms: Precision is about consistency - does the test give the same answer when repeated? Agreement is about consensus - do different people/tests agree? Accuracy is about correctness - does the test correctly identify disease? All three are needed for a trustworthy diagnostic test (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012).

References: The concepts of diagnostic test evaluation are thoroughly explained in epidemiological texts (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024; Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). For practical R implementations, see the Epidemiologist R Handbook (Batra et al., 2021).

6.4 Exercise 5.1: Precision of Quantitative Test Results

This section demonstrates how to measure the precision (reliability) of quantitative diagnostic tests (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). Precision measures how consistent results are when the same sample is tested multiple times.

What is this section doing?

This section measures how consistent and reliable a test is when you run it multiple times (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). Think of it like checking if a scale gives the same weight when you weigh something three times:

  1. Coefficient of Variation (CV): Measures how much the results vary relative to the average (like asking “how much do the three measurements differ?”)
  2. Correlation: Measures how similar results are between different test runs (like asking “if one run gives a high value, do the other runs also give high values?”)
  3. Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC): A more sophisticated measure that checks both accuracy and precision (like checking if the measurements are both consistent AND close to the true value)

In simple terms: We’re checking if the test is reliable - do you get similar results when you test the same sample multiple times? A good test should give consistent results.

nv_precision <- nv |>
  mutate(
    avg = rowMeans(across(c(eia1, eia2, eia3)), na.rm = TRUE),
    sd = matrixStats::rowSds(as.matrix(across(c(eia1, eia2, eia3))), na.rm = TRUE),
    cv = sd / avg
  )

mean_cv <- mean(nv_precision$cv, na.rm = TRUE)

6.5 Exercise 5.2: Agreement for Dichotomous Outcomes

This section demonstrates methods for measuring agreement between two tests or raters when outcomes are binary (yes/no, positive/negative) (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). Agreement measures are essential for evaluating diagnostic tests and inter-rater reliability.

What is this section doing?

This section measures how well two tests agree when the result is just “yes” or “no” (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012) (like positive/negative). Think of it like comparing two doctors’ diagnoses:

  1. Kappa: Measures agreement beyond what you’d expect by chance (like asking “do the doctors agree more than if they were just guessing?”)
  2. McNemar’s test: Checks if the tests disagree in a systematic way (like asking “when they disagree, is one test always more likely to say positive?”)
  3. Bootstrap confidence intervals: Uses computer simulation to estimate how confident we are in our kappa value

In simple terms: We’re checking if two tests (or two raters) give the same answer. High agreement means they usually agree; low agreement means they often disagree.

tbl_pcr_eia <- table(nv$pcr_c2, nv$eiafin_c2)
mcnemar_result <- mcnemar.test(tbl_pcr_eia)
kappa_unweighted <- irr::kappa2(
  nv |> select(pcr_c2, eiafin_c2),
  weight = "unweighted"
)

cohen_ci <- psych::cohen.kappa(tbl_pcr_eia)

kappa_boot <- boot(
  data = nv |> select(pcr_c2, eiafin_c2),
  statistic = function(data, index) {
    kappa2(data[index, ], weight = "unweighted")$value
  },
  R = 1000
)

boot_ci <- boot.ci(kappa_boot, type = c("perc", "basic"))

list(
  mcnemar = mcnemar_result,
  kappa = kappa_unweighted,
  cohen_ci = cohen_ci,
  kappa_boot_ci = boot_ci
)

6.6 Exercise 5.3: Agreement for Ordinal Outcomes

This section demonstrates agreement measures for ordinal (ordered categorical) outcomes, such as disease severity scales (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). Weighted kappa accounts for the ordering of categories.

What is this section doing?

This section measures agreement when results are in categories with an order (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012) (like “low”, “medium”, “high”). Think of it like comparing two teachers’ letter grades:

  1. Unweighted kappa: Treats all disagreements equally (like treating “A vs B” the same as “A vs F”)
  2. Weighted kappa: Gives more credit for being close (like giving more credit if one teacher says “B” and the other says “C” than if one says “A” and the other says “F”)

In simple terms: When results are ordered categories, we want to know if the tests agree, and we care more about being close than being far apart.

nv_ord <- nv |>
  mutate(
    pcr_c3 = case_when(
      is.na(pcr) ~ 0L,
      pcr >= 1 & pcr < 27 ~ 2L,
      pcr >= 27 & pcr <= 50 ~ 1L,
      TRUE ~ 0L

    eiafin_c3 = case_when(
      is.na(eiafin) ~ NA_integer_,
      eiafin < 0 ~ 0L,
      eiafin < 0.09 ~ 1L,
      eiafin < 0.11 ~ 2L,
      TRUE ~ 2L

  )

6.7 Exercise 5.4: Sensitivity and Specificity

This section demonstrates how to calculate sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values - the fundamental measures of diagnostic test accuracy (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). These measures are essential for evaluating any diagnostic test.

What is this section doing?

This section measures how good a test is at detecting disease (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). Think of it like evaluating a security system:

  1. Sensitivity: How good is the test at finding people who really have the disease? (like asking “does the alarm go off when there’s really a break-in?”)
  2. Specificity: How good is the test at correctly identifying people who don’t have the disease? (like asking “does the alarm stay quiet when there’s no break-in?”)
  3. Positive Predictive Value: If the test says positive, how likely is it that the person really has the disease?
  4. Negative Predictive Value: If the test says negative, how likely is it that the person really doesn’t have the disease?

In simple terms: We’re checking how accurate the test is - does it correctly identify who has the disease and who doesn’t?

6.8 Figure 5.4: Selecting a Cutpoint

What is this section doing?

This section shows how to choose the “cutoff” value that separates “positive” from “negative” test results. Think of it like setting the passing grade for an exam:

  • If you set it too low, almost everyone passes (high sensitivity, but many false positives)
  • If you set it too high, almost no one passes (high specificity, but many false negatives)
  • The cutpoint is where the two curves (diseased vs healthy) overlap - this is where you have to make a decision

In simple terms: We’re figuring out where to draw the line between “positive” and “negative” test results. The best cutpoint balances finding true cases while avoiding false alarms.

6.9 Figure 5.5 and 5.6: ROC Analyses

What is this section doing?

This section creates ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) curves, which show how well a test performs across all possible cutpoints. Think of it like testing a radar system at different sensitivity settings:

  1. ROC curve: Plots sensitivity vs (1 - specificity) for all possible cutpoints
  2. Area Under the Curve (AUC): A single number that summarizes how good the test is overall (1.0 = perfect, 0.5 = no better than guessing)
  3. Cutpoint selection: Helps you choose the best threshold based on whether you care more about finding all cases or avoiding false alarms

In simple terms: The ROC curve shows you all the trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity. A curve that goes up and to the left is better - it means you can have high sensitivity without sacrificing too much specificity.

6.10 Exercise 5.7: Likelihood Ratios

This section demonstrates likelihood ratios, which quantify how much a test result changes the probability of disease (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). Likelihood ratios are particularly useful for interpreting test results in clinical practice.

What is this section doing?

This section calculates likelihood ratios (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012), which tell you how much a test result changes the probability of disease. Think of it like updating your belief based on new evidence:

  1. Positive Likelihood Ratio (LR+): How much more likely is disease if the test is positive? (like asking “if the test is positive, how much does that increase the chance of disease?”)
  2. Negative Likelihood Ratio (LR-): How much less likely is disease if the test is negative? (like asking “if the test is negative, how much does that decrease the chance of disease?”)

In simple terms: Likelihood ratios help you interpret test results in context. A high LR+ means a positive test is strong evidence for disease. A low LR- means a negative test is strong evidence against disease.

thresholds <- c(-1, 0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.30, 0.70, 1.50)

lr_results <- purrr::map_dfr(thresholds, function(cutpoint) {
  classification <- factor(if_else(nv_clean$eiafin >= cutpoint, 1L, 0L, missing = NA_integer_), levels = c(0, 1))
  outcome <- factor(nv_clean$gs, levels = c(0, 1))
  tbl <- table(outcome, classification)
  if (any(tbl == 0)) {
    return(tibble(
      cutpoint = cutpoint,
      sensitivity = NA_real_,
      specificity = NA_real_,
      lr_pos = NA_real_,
      lr_neg = NA_real_
    ))
  }
  diag <- suppressWarnings(epi.tests(tbl))
  tibble(
    cutpoint = cutpoint,
    sensitivity = diag$detail$se,
    specificity = diag$detail$sp,
    lr_pos = diag$detail$lr.pos,
    lr_neg = diag$detail$lr.neg
  )
})

6.11 Exercise 5.8: Parallel and Serial Interpretation

This section demonstrates how to combine multiple diagnostic tests using parallel or serial testing strategies (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). These strategies allow you to optimize sensitivity or specificity depending on your clinical needs.

What is this section doing?

This section shows two ways to combine multiple tests (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012):

  1. Parallel testing: A person is considered positive if ANY test is positive (like “if either test says positive, we treat it as positive”). This increases sensitivity but decreases specificity.
  2. Serial testing: A person is only considered positive if ALL tests are positive (like “both tests must be positive”). This increases specificity but decreases sensitivity.

In simple terms: If you have two tests, you can use them together. Parallel testing is like “better safe than sorry” - you catch more cases but get more false alarms. Serial testing is more conservative - you’re more certain when you say positive, but you might miss some cases.

  )

6.12 Summary

This chapter has covered the fundamental concepts for evaluating diagnostic tests in epidemiology (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024):

6.12.1 Key Concepts

  1. Precision (Reliability): Measures how consistent a test is when repeated on the same sample. High precision means consistent results (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012).

  2. Agreement: Measures how well different tests or raters agree. Essential for inter-rater reliability and test validation (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003).

  3. Sensitivity and Specificity: Fundamental measures of diagnostic accuracy:

    • Sensitivity: Ability to detect true cases (true positive rate)
    • Specificity: Ability to correctly identify non-cases (true negative rate)

  4. ROC Curves: Graphical representation of the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity at different cutpoints (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012).

  5. Likelihood Ratios: Quantify how much a test result changes disease probability, useful for clinical interpretation (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003).

  6. Parallel and Serial Testing: Strategies for combining multiple tests to optimize sensitivity or specificity (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012).

6.12.2 Practical Applications

These methods are essential for: - Validating new diagnostic tests - Comparing different diagnostic methods - Setting appropriate test cutpoints - Interpreting test results in clinical practice - Quality assurance in diagnostic laboratories

For more detailed examples and R code implementations, see the Epidemiologist R Handbook (Batra et al., 2021) and R4SME resources (Mazzella, n.d.).
