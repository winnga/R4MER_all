4.1 Overview

This chapter covers sampling methods in epidemiological research, including probability-of-selection calculations, sampling weights, survey-weighted analyses, sample size calculations, and power estimation through simulation. Understanding sampling is fundamental to conducting valid epidemiological research, as the way we select participants directly affects the conclusions we can draw from our studies (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024).

Note

Note: This chapter reproduces and expands upon the analyses from the original Methods in Epidemiologic Research (MER) Chapter 2 on Sampling (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). The content focuses on probability-of-selection and weight calculations, survey-weighted incidence estimates under alternative designs, sample-size adjustments, and simulation-based power estimation.

4.3 Theoretical Background

Understanding Sampling in Epidemiology

Before we calculate sampling weights and analyze survey data, let’s understand the fundamentals of sampling. This knowledge is essential for interpreting epidemiological research correctly (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003).

4.3.1 What is Sampling?

Sampling means selecting a subset of people from a larger population to study. Instead of studying everyone (which would be expensive and time-consuming), we study a sample and use it to make inferences about the whole population (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012).

Real-world example: Imagine you want to know what percentage of people in your city have been vaccinated. You can’t ask all 1 million residents - that would take forever! Instead, you might randomly select 1,000 people and ask them. If 850 out of those 1,000 are vaccinated, you estimate that 85% of the entire city is vaccinated.

4.3.2 Why Sampling Matters

Proper sampling ensures that: - Your results represent the population you care about - You can make valid statistical inferences - Your study is feasible and cost-effective - You can estimate how certain you are about your results

4.3.3 Probability of Selection

The probability of selection is the chance that any individual (or group) was chosen to be in your study (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). If you randomly select 100 people from 1000, each person has a 100/1000 = 0.1 (10%) chance of being selected.

Key concept: When everyone has the same probability of being selected, we call it “simple random sampling.” But in real research, we often use more complex designs where different people have different probabilities.

Example: In a study of schools, you might: 1. First randomly select 10 schools (each school has a 10/100 = 10% chance) 2. Then randomly select 20 students from each school (each student has a 20/500 = 4% chance within their school) 3. The overall probability for a student = 10% × 4% = 0.4%

4.3.4 Sampling Weights

Sampling weights adjust your data to account for how people were selected (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). If some groups were more likely to be selected than others, you need to “down-weight” them (give them less importance) in your analysis. The weight is usually 1 divided by the probability of selection.

Why weights matter: Without weights, groups that were oversampled (selected more often) would have too much influence on your results. Weights “balance” the data back to what it would look like if everyone had an equal chance of being selected.

Simple example: - Person A had a 10% chance of selection → weight = 1/0.10 = 10 - Person B had a 5% chance of selection → weight = 1/0.05 = 20

Person B gets a higher weight because they were less likely to be selected, so they “represent” more people in the population.

4.3.5 Design Effects

A design effect tells you how much your complex sampling design (like clustering or stratification) affects your results compared to simple random sampling (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). A design effect of 2 means your standard errors are twice as large as they would be with simple random sampling.

What this means: Complex sampling designs usually give you less precise estimates (wider confidence intervals) than simple random sampling. The design effect quantifies this loss of precision.

Example: - Simple random sampling: 95% confidence interval = 10% to 20% - With design effect of 2: 95% confidence interval = 5% to 25% (much wider!)

4.3.6 Why This Matters

If you ignore sampling design, you might: - Get wrong confidence intervals (too narrow) - making you think you’re more certain than you really are - Make incorrect conclusions about statistical significance - thinking something is significant when it’s not, or vice versa - Underestimate or overestimate disease rates - leading to wrong public health decisions

Bottom line: Properly accounting for sampling design is not optional - it’s essential for valid research (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024).

4.5 Probability of Selection and Weight Calculations

This section demonstrates how to calculate the probability that each individual (or group) was selected for the study, and then how to create sampling weights from those probabilities (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). These calculations are fundamental to proper survey analysis.

Understanding Probability of Selection Calculations

What we’re doing: We’re figuring out how likely each person was to be included in our study, then using that to create weights that adjust for unequal selection probabilities.

Why it matters: If some people were more likely to be selected than others, we need to account for that in our analysis. Otherwise, our results will be biased toward the groups that were oversampled.

The process: 1. Calculate probability of selection at each stage (e.g., municipality, then family, then individual) 2. Multiply probabilities across stages to get overall probability 3. Calculate weight as 1 divided by probability 4. Use these weights in all subsequent analyses

# Load data if available
if (file.exists(brazil_file)) {
  # Read the Stata data file
  brazil <- read_dta(brazil_file)
  
  cat("Data loaded successfully!\n")
  cat("Number of observations: ", nrow(brazil), "\n")
  cat("Number of variables: ", ncol(brazil), "\n\n")
  
  # Display first few rows to see what we're working with
  cat("First few rows of data:\n")
  print(head(brazil, 3))
  
  # Calculate probability of selection for cistern groups
  # This is a multi-stage process:
  # 1. Probability of selecting municipality
  # 2. Probability of selecting cistern group within municipality
  # 3. Probability of selecting family within cistern group
  # 4. Overall probability = product of all stages
  
  cat("\n--- Calculating Probabilities of Selection ---\n")
  
  # Example calculation structure (actual implementation depends on data structure)
  # Step 1: Calculate probability of selection for cistern groups within each municipality
  if ("cistern" %in% names(brazil) && "mun" %in% names(brazil)) {
    brazil <- brazil %>%
      group_by(mun) %>%
      mutate(
        # Count how many people in each cistern group were sampled
        n_sampled_cistern = sum(cistern == 1, na.rm = TRUE),
        n_sampled_no_cistern = sum(cistern == 0, na.rm = TRUE),
        # Get total eligible in each group (if available in data)
        # Note: This assumes the data has variables like m_cistern_const and m_cistern_elig
        # Probability = number sampled / number eligible
        psel_cistern = ifelse(
          "m_cistern_const" %in% names(brazil) & cistern == 1,
          n_sampled_cistern / m_cistern_const,
          NA_real_
        )
      ) %>%
      ungroup()
    
    cat("✓ Calculated probabilities for cistern groups\n")
  }
  
  # Step 2: Calculate probability of family selection
  if ("fam" %in% names(brazil) && "fam_n" %in% names(brazil)) {
    brazil <- brazil %>%
      group_by(fam) %>%
      mutate(
        # Number of people from this family in the sample
        n_sampled_fam = n(),
        # Probability = sampled / total family size
        psel_fam = n_sampled_fam / first(fam_n)
      ) %>%
      ungroup()
    
    cat("✓ Calculated probabilities for family selection\n")
  }
  
  # Step 3: Calculate overall probability and weights
  if ("psel_cistern" %in% names(brazil) && "psel_fam" %in% names(brazil)) {
    brazil <- brazil %>%
      mutate(
        # Overall probability = product of all stage probabilities
        psel = psel_cistern * psel_fam,
        # Weight = 1 / probability (people with lower probability get higher weight)
        wt = 1 / psel,
        # Cap weights at reasonable values (sometimes probabilities can be > 1 due to data issues)
        wt = ifelse(psel > 1, 1, wt),
        wt = ifelse(is.na(wt) | !is.finite(wt), 1, wt)
      )
    
    cat("✓ Calculated overall probabilities and weights\n\n")
    
    # Display summary of weights
    cat("Summary of sampling weights:\n")
    print(summary(brazil$wt))
    
    cat("\n✓ Weight calculation complete!\n")
  } else {
    cat("⚠ Note: Some required variables may be missing. Weight calculation may be incomplete.\n")
    cat("Expected variables: cistern, mun, fam, fam_n, m_cistern_const\n")
  }
  
} else {
  cat("⚠ Data file not found. Please ensure brazil.dta is in the data directory.\n")
  cat("Expected location: ", brazil_file, "\n")
  cat("\nFor demonstration purposes, here's what the weight calculation process looks like:\n\n")
  
  # Create a simple example to illustrate the concept
  example_data <- data.frame(
    municipality = rep(1:2, each = 10),
    cistern = rep(c(1, 0), 10),
    family = rep(1:5, each = 2),
    family_size = rep(c(4, 5, 3, 6, 4), each = 2)
  )
  
  example_data <- example_data %>%
    group_by(municipality, cistern) %>%
    mutate(
      n_sampled = n(),
      eligible_pop = ifelse(cistern == 1, 100, 200),  # Example: 100 with cistern, 200 without
      psel_stage1 = n_sampled / eligible_pop
    ) %>%
    group_by(family) %>%
    mutate(
      n_sampled_fam = n(),
      psel_stage2 = n_sampled_fam / first(family_size),
      psel_overall = psel_stage1 * psel_stage2,
      weight = 1 / psel_overall
    ) %>%
    ungroup()
  
  cat("Example calculation:\n")
  print(head(example_data, 6))
  
  cat("\nKey points:\n")
  cat("1. Probability at each stage = sampled / eligible\n")
  cat("2. Overall probability = product of all stages\n")
  cat("3. Weight = 1 / overall probability\n")
  cat("4. Higher weights compensate for lower selection probabilities\n")
}

4.6 Survey-Weighted Analyses

This section demonstrates how to perform survey-weighted analyses using different sampling designs (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Batra et al., 2021). The survey package in R is specifically designed for this purpose and handles all the complex calculations automatically.

Understanding Survey-Weighted Analysis

When you have complex sampling (like clusters or strata), you can’t just analyze data as if everyone had an equal chance of being selected. Survey-weighted analysis accounts for:

  • Stratification: Different groups sampled separately (e.g., sampling from urban and rural areas separately)
  • Clustering: People grouped together (like families or villages) - people in the same cluster tend to be more similar
  • Weights: Different probabilities of selection - some people represent more people in the population

The survey package workflow: 1. Define your survey design with svydesign() - tell R about your strata, clusters, and weights 2. Use survey-specific functions like svymean(), svyglm(), etc. instead of regular R functions 3. R automatically accounts for your complex design in all calculations

Why this matters: If you use regular R functions (like mean() or glm()) on complex survey data, you’ll get wrong answers. The survey package gives you correct answers that account for your sampling design.

# This section demonstrates different survey design configurations
# Each configuration accounts for different aspects of the sampling design

if (file.exists(brazil_smpl_file)) {
  # Load the sampling data (which should already have weights calculated)
  brazil_smpl <- read_dta(brazil_smpl_file)
  
  cat("=== Survey-Weighted Analysis Examples ===\n\n")
  
  # Example 1: Simple Random Sample (SRS)
  # This assumes everyone had an equal chance of selection
  cat("1. Simple Random Sample (SRS) Design\n")
  cat("   Assumes: Everyone had equal probability of selection\n")
  
  if ("diarr" %in% names(brazil_smpl) && "id" %in% names(brazil_smpl)) {
    design_srs <- svydesign(
      id = ~id,           # Individual identifier
      data = brazil_smpl,
      weights = NULL      # No weights (equal probability)
    )
    
    # Calculate proportion with diarrhea
    prop_diarr_srs <- svymean(~diarr, design_srs, na.rm = TRUE)
    cat("   Proportion with diarrhea (SRS):\n")
    print(prop_diarr_srs)
    cat("   Standard error: ", SE(prop_diarr_srs)[1], "\n\n")
  }
  
  # Example 2: Stratified Sample
  # Accounts for stratification (e.g., sampling from different municipalities separately)
  cat("2. Stratified Sample Design\n")
  cat("   Accounts for: Different groups (strata) sampled separately\n")
  
  if ("mun" %in% names(brazil_smpl)) {
    design_stratified <- svydesign(
      id = ~id,
      strata = ~mun,      # Stratification variable (municipality)
      data = brazil_smpl,
      weights = NULL
    )
    
    if ("diarr" %in% names(brazil_smpl)) {
      prop_diarr_strat <- svymean(~diarr, design_stratified, na.rm = TRUE)
      cat("   Proportion with diarrhea (stratified):\n")
      print(prop_diarr_strat)
      cat("   Standard error: ", SE(prop_diarr_strat)[1], "\n\n")
      
      # Compare to SRS
      if (exists("prop_diarr_srs")) {
        cat("   Comparison:\n")
        cat("   SRS SE: ", SE(prop_diarr_srs)[1], "\n")
        cat("   Stratified SE: ", SE(prop_diarr_strat)[1], "\n")
        cat("   Design effect: ", (SE(prop_diarr_strat)[1] / SE(prop_diarr_srs)[1])^2, "\n\n")
      }
    }
  }
  
  # Example 3: Stratified with Weights
  # Accounts for both stratification AND unequal selection probabilities
  cat("3. Stratified Sample with Weights\n")
  cat("   Accounts for: Stratification + unequal selection probabilities\n")
  
  if ("wt" %in% names(brazil_smpl) && "mun" %in% names(brazil_smpl)) {
    design_weighted <- svydesign(
      id = ~id,
      strata = ~mun,
      weights = ~wt,      # Sampling weights
      data = brazil_smpl
    )
    
    if ("diarr" %in% names(brazil_smpl)) {
      prop_diarr_weighted <- svymean(~diarr, design_weighted, na.rm = TRUE)
      cat("   Proportion with diarrhea (stratified + weighted):\n")
      print(prop_diarr_weighted)
      cat("   Standard error: ", SE(prop_diarr_weighted)[1], "\n\n")
    }
  }
  
  # Example 4: Clustered Sample
  # Accounts for clustering (e.g., families or villages)
  cat("4. Clustered Sample Design\n")
  cat("   Accounts for: People grouped in clusters (families, villages)\n")
  cat("   Note: People in same cluster tend to be more similar\n")
  
  if ("fam" %in% names(brazil_smpl)) {
    design_clustered <- svydesign(
      id = ~fam,          # Cluster identifier (family)
      data = brazil_smpl,
      weights = NULL
    )
    
    if ("diarr" %in% names(brazil_smpl)) {
      prop_diarr_clust <- svymean(~diarr, design_clustered, na.rm = TRUE)
      cat("   Proportion with diarrhea (clustered):\n")
      print(prop_diarr_clust)
      cat("   Standard error: ", SE(prop_diarr_clust)[1], "\n\n")
      
      # Design effect for clustering
      if (exists("prop_diarr_srs")) {
        deff_cluster <- (SE(prop_diarr_clust)[1] / SE(prop_diarr_srs)[1])^2
        cat("   Design effect (clustering): ", deff_cluster, "\n")
        cat("   Interpretation: Clustering makes SE ", round(sqrt(deff_cluster), 2), 
            " times larger\n\n")
      }
    }
  }
  
  # Example 5: Full Complex Design
  # Accounts for stratification, clustering, AND weights
  cat("5. Full Complex Design (Stratified + Clustered + Weighted)\n")
  cat("   Accounts for: All aspects of complex sampling\n")
  cat("   This is usually what you need for real survey data!\n")
  
  if (all(c("fam", "mun", "wt") %in% names(brazil_smpl))) {
    design_full <- svydesign(
      id = ~fam,          # Cluster (family)
      strata = ~mun,      # Stratum (municipality)
      weights = ~wt,      # Sampling weights
      data = brazil_smpl
    )
    
    if ("diarr" %in% names(brazil_smpl)) {
      prop_diarr_full <- svymean(~diarr, design_full, na.rm = TRUE)
      cat("   Proportion with diarrhea (full design):\n")
      print(prop_diarr_full)
      cat("   Standard error: ", SE(prop_diarr_full)[1], "\n\n")
      
      # Calculate design effect
      if (exists("prop_diarr_srs")) {
        deff_full <- (SE(prop_diarr_full)[1] / SE(prop_diarr_srs)[1])^2
        cat("   Design effect: ", deff_full, "\n")
        cat("   This means the complex design makes your SE ", 
            round(sqrt(deff_full), 2), " times larger than SRS\n")
        cat("   You need ", round(deff_full, 1), " times more people to get same precision as SRS\n\n")
      }
    }
  }
  
  cat("=== Key Takeaways ===\n")
  cat("1. Always use svydesign() to define your survey structure\n")
  cat("2. Use survey functions (svymean, svyglm, etc.) not regular R functions\n")
  cat("3. Design effects tell you how much precision you lose from complex sampling\n")
  cat("4. Ignoring sampling design gives wrong (usually too optimistic) results!\n")
  
} else {
  cat("⚠ Sampling data file not found. Showing conceptual examples instead.\n\n")

  cat("1. Simple Random Sample:\n")
  cat("   svydesign(id = ~id, data = data)\n")
  cat("   → Everyone has equal chance\n\n")

  cat("   → Accounts for unequal selection probabilities\n\n")
  
  cat("4. Clustered:\n")
  cat("   svydesign(id = ~village, data = data)\n")
  cat("   → People grouped in villages\n\n")
  
  cat("5. Full Design:\n")
  cat("   svydesign(id = ~village, strata = ~region, weights = ~wt, data = data)\n")

4.7 Sample Size Calculations

This section covers sample size calculations for epidemiological studies (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003; Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). Calculating the right sample size is crucial - too small and you might miss important effects, too large and you waste resources.

Understanding Sample Size Calculations

What we’re doing: Figuring out how many people we need to study to have a good chance of detecting an effect if it exists.

Key concepts: - Power: The probability of detecting an effect if it really exists (usually we want 80% or 90%) - Effect size: How big a difference we want to be able to detect (e.g., 15% vs 10% disease rate) - Significance level: How sure we want to be that we’re not making a false positive (usually 5%)

The trade-off: - Larger sample = more power = better chance of detecting effects - But larger sample = more expensive and time-consuming - We want the smallest sample that gives us enough power

Example: If we’re comparing two treatments and expect one to have 15% success rate and the other 10%, we need to calculate how many people in each group to have 80% power to detect this 5% difference.

4.8 Power Calculation by Simulation

This section demonstrates power calculation through simulation, which is particularly useful for complex study designs where mathematical formulas don’t exist or are too complicated (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). Simulation is like “testing” your study design thousands of times on computer-generated data.

Why Simulate Power?

Sometimes the math for power calculations gets too complicated, especially with: - Complex sampling designs (multiple levels of clustering) - Non-standard statistical models - Multiple comparisons or complex hypotheses - When assumptions of standard formulas are violated

How simulation works: 1. Create “fake” data that matches what you expect to see (under both null and alternative hypotheses) 2. Run your planned analysis on this fake data 3. Repeat steps 1-2 thousands of times 4. Count how often you get a “significant” result 5. That proportion is your power!

Example: If you run 1000 simulations and get significant results 800 times, your power is 80%.

Advantages: - Works for any design, no matter how complex - You can see the full distribution of results, not just power - Easy to understand and explain - Can account for real-world complications (missing data, etc.)

# Function to simulate clustered binary data
simulate_clustered_data <- function(n_clusters, n_per_cluster, p, icc) {
  # Generate cluster-level random effects
  cluster_effects <- rnorm(n_clusters, mean = 0, sd = sqrt(icc))
  
  # Convert to probabilities on logit scale
  logit_p_cluster <- qlogis(p) + cluster_effects
  
  # Generate individual outcomes
  outcomes <- numeric()
  cluster_ids <- numeric()
  
  for (i in 1:n_clusters) {
    p_cluster <- plogis(logit_p_cluster[i])
    cluster_outcomes <- rbinom(n_per_cluster, 1, p_cluster)
    outcomes <- c(outcomes, cluster_outcomes)
    cluster_ids <- c(cluster_ids, rep(i, n_per_cluster))
  }
  
  data.frame(
    outcome = outcomes,
    cluster = cluster_ids,
    group = rep(c("control", "treatment"), each = n_clusters * n_per_cluster)
  )
}

p_values <- numeric(n_simulations)

4.9 Summary

This chapter has covered the fundamental concepts and methods for sampling in epidemiological research (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003):

4.9.1 Key Concepts Learned

  1. Probability of Selection: The chance that each individual or group was included in the study. This must be calculated at each stage of multi-stage sampling.

  2. Sampling Weights: Adjustments that account for unequal selection probabilities. Weights = 1 / probability of selection, ensuring that oversampled groups don’t dominate results.

  3. Survey-Weighted Analysis: Using specialized R functions (from the survey package) that properly account for complex sampling designs including stratification, clustering, and weights (Batra et al., 2021).

  4. Design Effects: Quantify how much complex sampling reduces precision compared to simple random sampling. A design effect of 2 means you need twice as many people for the same precision.

  5. Sample Size Calculations: Determining how many participants are needed to have adequate power (usually 80%) to detect meaningful effects. This must be done before starting a study (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003).

  6. Power Simulation: Using computer simulation to estimate power for complex designs where mathematical formulas are unavailable or too complicated (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012).

4.9.2 Practical Takeaways

  • Always account for sampling design in your analyses. Ignoring it leads to incorrect conclusions.
  • Use the survey package in R for all analyses of complex survey data (Batra et al., 2021).
  • Calculate sample size before starting your study to ensure you have adequate power.
  • Consider design effects when planning studies - complex designs require larger samples.
  • Simulation is powerful for understanding power in complex situations.
