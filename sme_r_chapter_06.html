7.2 Theoretical Background

Understanding Measures of Association

Before calculating associations, let’s understand what they mean and why we need different types. These concepts are fundamental to epidemiological research and are covered extensively in standard texts (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024; Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003):

7.2.1 What is an Association?

An association is a relationship between two things. In health research, we often ask: “Is there a relationship between an exposure (like smoking) and an outcome (like disease)?”

Important: Association doesn’t mean causation! Just because two things are related doesn’t mean one causes the other. But finding an association is the first step toward understanding causation (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024).

7.2.2 Types of Measures

7.2.3 1. Risk Ratio (Relative Risk)

Risk Ratio asks: “How many times more likely is the outcome in the exposed group compared to the unexposed group?” (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003)

  • RR = 1: No difference (exposed and unexposed have the same risk)
  • RR = 2: Exposed group is twice as likely to have the outcome
  • RR = 0.5: Exposed group is half as likely (protected)

Example: If smokers have a 20% risk of lung cancer and non-smokers have a 2% risk, the risk ratio is 20%/2% = 10. Smokers are 10 times more likely to get lung cancer.

7.2.4 2. Odds Ratio

Odds Ratio is similar to risk ratio but uses odds instead of risk. It’s especially useful when you can’t calculate risk directly (like in case-control studies) (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024).

  • OR = 1: No association
  • OR > 1: Positive association (exposure increases odds)
  • OR < 1: Negative association (exposure decreases odds)

When to use: Odds ratios are used in case-control studies and logistic regression. When the outcome is rare, odds ratios approximate risk ratios.

7.2.5 3. Incidence Rate Ratio

Incidence Rate Ratio compares how fast events happen in different groups. It’s like comparing speed (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003).

  • IRR = 1: Same rate in both groups
  • IRR = 2: Events happen twice as fast in the exposed group

Example: If Group A has 10 cases per 100 person-years and Group B has 5 cases per 100 person-years, the IRR is 2. Events happen twice as fast in Group A.

7.2.6 Why Different Measures?

Different situations call for different measures: - Risk Ratio: Best for cohort studies with complete follow-up - Odds Ratio: Best for case-control studies or when using logistic regression - Incidence Rate Ratio: Best when people are followed for different amounts of time

7.2.7 Interpreting Results

  • Confidence intervals: Tell us how certain we are. If the interval doesn’t include 1, the association is statistically significant.
  • Effect size: A ratio of 2 is a stronger effect than 1.5, but we also need to consider the baseline risk.
  • Clinical significance: A statistically significant result might not be clinically important if the effect is small.

In simple terms: Measures of association tell us how strongly two things are related. Risk ratios compare risks, odds ratios compare odds, and rate ratios compare speeds. All help us understand if an exposure affects an outcome, but we need to choose the right measure for our study design.

  1. Loading data: Opening files with information about exposures (like smoking) and outcomes (like low birth weight)

7.4 Table 6.3: Risk and Odds Ratios

What is this section doing?

This section calculates risk ratios and odds ratios, which measure how much more likely an outcome is in one group compared to another. Think of it like comparing two groups:

  1. Risk Ratio (RR): How many times more likely is the outcome in the exposed group? (like “are people with cisterns 2 times more likely to get diarrhea?”)
  2. Odds Ratio (OR): Similar to risk ratio but uses odds instead of risk (useful when you can’t calculate risk directly)

In simple terms: We’re asking “how much stronger is the association?” A ratio of 1 means no difference. A ratio of 2 means the exposed group is twice as likely to have the outcome.

brazil <- read_dta(file.path(data_dir, "brazil.dta")) |>
  mutate(
    diarr = haven::as_factor(diarr),
    cistern = haven::as_factor(cistern),
    diarr_flag = if_else(diarr == "Yes", 1L, 0L, missing = NA_integer_),
    cistern_flag = if_else(cistern == "Yes", 1L, 0L, missing = NA_integer_)
  )

tab_brazil <- table(brazil$diarr_flag, brazil$cistern_flag)
cs_brazil <- epi.2by2(
  dat = tab_brazil,
  method = "cohort.count",
  conf.level = 0.95,

7.5 Table 6.4: Incidence Rate Ratio

What is this section doing?

This section calculates incidence rate ratios, which compare how fast events happen in different groups. Think of it like comparing speed:

  • Incidence Rate: How many cases per person-time (like “5 cases per 100 person-years”)
  • Incidence Rate Ratio (IRR): How many times faster do events happen in one group vs another?

In simple terms: We’re comparing how quickly disease occurs in different groups. An IRR of 2 means events happen twice as fast in the exposed group.

calc_iri <- function(cases_exp, pt_exp, cases_unexp, pt_unexp) {
  rate_exp <- cases_exp / pt_exp
  rate_unexp <- cases_unexp / pt_unexp
  irr <- rate_exp / rate_unexp
  se_log <- sqrt(1 / cases_exp + 1 / cases_unexp)
  ci_tb <- exp(log(irr) + c(-1, 1) * 1.96 * se_log)

  tibble(
    rate_exp = rate_exp,
    rate_unexp = rate_unexp,
    irr = irr,
    ci_lower_tb = ci_tb[1],
    ci_upper_tb = ci_tb[2]
  )
}

iri_counts <- calc_iri(131, 44, 250, 236)

7.6 Example 6.1: Cohort Study Measures

What is this section doing?

This section analyzes a cohort study, where we follow groups of people over time to see who develops the outcome. Think of it like a long-term study:

  • We start with exposed and unexposed groups
  • We follow them over time
  • We see who gets the outcome
  • We calculate how much more likely the outcome is in the exposed group

In simple terms: We’re following people over time to see if exposure leads to the outcome, and calculating how strong that relationship is.

low_bw_tab <- table(low_bw$low_bw, low_bw$smk)

cs_low_bw <- epi.2by2(

7.7 Example 6.2: Confidence Intervals

What is this section doing?

This section calculates confidence intervals for our measures of association. Think of it like giving a range instead of a single number:

  • Exact confidence intervals: Uses precise mathematical formulas
  • Test-based confidence intervals: Uses statistical test results to create the interval

In simple terms: Instead of just saying “the risk ratio is 2.0”, we say “the risk ratio is 2.0, and we’re 95% confident it’s somewhere between 1.5 and 2.5”. This tells us how certain we are about our estimate.

cs_exact <- epi.2by2(
