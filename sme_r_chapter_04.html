5.2 Theoretical Background

Understanding Confidence Intervals and Standardization

Before we analyze the data, let’s understand what confidence intervals and standardization mean. These concepts are fundamental to epidemiological research and are covered extensively in standard textbooks (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024; Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003; Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012).

5.2.1 Confidence Intervals: The Range of Uncertainty

Imagine you’re trying to guess someone’s height. You measure them and get 170 cm. But you know your measurement isn’t perfect - maybe it’s off by a few centimeters. A confidence interval tells you: “I’m 95% confident the true height is between 168 and 172 cm.”

Key ideas: - Point estimate: Your best guess (like 170 cm) - Confidence interval: A range where you’re confident the true value lies (like 168-172 cm) - Confidence level: How confident you are (usually 95%, meaning if you did this 100 times, you’d be right about 95 times)

5.2.2 Why We Need Confidence Intervals

When you calculate that 15% of people got sick, that’s based on your sample. But what if you’d sampled different people? You might have gotten 14% or 16%. The confidence interval accounts for this uncertainty - it says “we’re pretty sure the true percentage is between 13% and 17%.”

5.2.3 Types of Confidence Intervals

  1. Approximate (Normal): Uses a mathematical shortcut that works well with large samples - like estimating with a quick calculation
  2. Exact (Binomial): Uses precise math that works even with small samples - like doing the exact calculation
  3. Poisson: For rates (like cases per person-year), accounts for the fact that we’re counting events over time

5.2.4 Standardization: Making Fair Comparisons

Imagine comparing test scores between two classes. Class A has mostly older students, Class B has mostly younger students. Even if Class A scores higher, is it because they’re better, or just because they’re older? Standardization adjusts for this.

Direct Standardization: Apply each group’s rates to a “standard” population. It’s like asking: “If both groups had the same age distribution, what would their rates be?” This lets you compare groups fairly.

Indirect Standardization: Compare observed cases to what you’d expect based on a reference population. It’s like asking: “Did this group have more cases than we’d expect based on a standard population?”

5.2.5 Why Standardization Matters

Without standardization, you might think one treatment is better when really it’s just that the groups had different characteristics (like age). Standardization removes these differences so you can make fair comparisons.

In simple terms: Confidence intervals tell us how certain we are about our estimates - they give us a range, not just a single number. Standardization helps us compare groups fairly by accounting for differences in their characteristics (like age distributions). Both are essential for trustworthy research (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024).

References for this section: The concepts of confidence intervals are thoroughly explained in statistical textbooks (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003), while standardization methods are detailed in epidemiological texts (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024). For practical R implementations, see the Epidemiologist R Handbook (Batra et al., 2021).

5.4 Confidence Intervals for Risks and Rates

This section demonstrates how to calculate confidence intervals for disease risks and rates, which quantify the uncertainty in our estimates (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003; Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). Understanding confidence intervals is crucial for interpreting epidemiological data correctly.

What is this section doing?

This section calculates confidence intervals - which tell us how certain we are about our estimates (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). Think of it like polling: if 60% of people say they like pizza, the confidence interval tells you the range (like 55-65%) where the true answer probably lies:

  1. Approximate confidence intervals: Uses a mathematical shortcut that works well with large samples (like estimating with a quick calculation)
  2. Exact confidence intervals: Uses precise math that works even with small samples (like doing the exact calculation)
  3. Poisson confidence intervals: For rates (like cases per person-year), accounts for the fact that we’re counting events over time

In simple terms: We’re calculating not just “what percentage got sick” but also “how confident are we in that number?” The confidence interval gives us a range - we’re pretty sure the true answer is somewhere in that range (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003).

Why this matters: Without confidence intervals, you only have a single number (like “15% got sick”). But that number is based on your sample - if you’d sampled different people, you might have gotten 14% or 16%. The confidence interval accounts for this sampling variability and tells you the range where the true population value likely falls (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012).

five_to_nine <- gi_surv |> filter(age_c20_code == 1)

tab_ill_diarr <- five_to_nine |>
  tabyl(ill, ill_diarr)

approx_ci_ill <- prop.test(sum(five_to_nine$ill), nrow(five_to_nine))
approx_ci_diarr <- prop.test(sum(five_to_nine$ill_diarr), nrow(five_to_nine))

exact_ci_ill <- binom.test(sum(five_to_nine$ill), nrow(five_to_nine))
exact_ci_diarr <- binom.test(sum(five_to_nine$ill_diarr), nrow(five_to_nine))

poisson_cases <- sum(five_to_nine$ill_episodes)
poisson_time <- sum(five_to_nine$dar)
poisson_ci <- pois.exact(poisson_cases, pt = poisson_time)

5.5 Standardized Risks

This section demonstrates standardization methods, which are essential for making fair comparisons between groups with different characteristics (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024). Standardization adjusts for confounding factors (like age) that could make comparisons misleading.

What is this section doing?

This section “standardizes” rates to make fair comparisons between groups (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). Think of it like comparing test scores: if one class has older students and another has younger students, you need to account for age to make a fair comparison:

  1. Direct standardization: We apply the age-specific rates from each study to a standard population (like asking “what would the rate be if all studies had the same age distribution?”)
  2. Indirect standardization: We compare observed cases to what we’d expect based on a reference population (like asking “did this study have more or fewer cases than we’d expect?”)

In simple terms: When comparing disease rates between studies, the studies might have different age distributions. Standardization adjusts for this so we can make fair comparisons - like comparing apples to apples instead of apples to oranges (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024).

When to use each method: - Direct standardization: Use when you have detailed age-specific rates for all groups you want to compare. It’s more precise but requires more data (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012). - Indirect standardization: Use when you only have overall rates but want to compare to a standard population. It’s simpler but less precise (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003).

# Direct standardization: applying each study's age-specific rates to a standard population
# Method described in: Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn (2012); Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray (2024)
# It's like asking "if all studies had the same age mix, what would their rates be?"
# This lets us compare studies fairly even if they have different age distributions
# 
# Step-by-step process:
# 1. Calculate age-specific rates for each study
# 2. Apply those rates to a standard population
# 3. Sum up to get standardized rate for each study
# 4. Now rates are comparable because they're all based on the same age distribution
std_population <- collapsed |>
  group_by(age_c4_code) |>
  summarise(std_n = sum(n), .groups = "drop")

direct_standardized <- collapsed |>
  left_join(std_population, by = "age_c4_code") |>
  mutate(rate = ill / n) |>
  group_by(study) |>
  summarise(
    crude_rate = sum(ill) / sum(n),
    std_rate = sum(rate * std_n) / sum(std_n),
    .groups = "drop"
  )

# Indirect standardization: comparing observed cases to expected cases based on a reference
# Method described in: Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn (2012); Kirkwood & Sterne (2003)
# It's like asking "did this study have more cases than we'd expect based on a standard population?"
# Useful when we don't have detailed age breakdowns for each study
#
# Step-by-step process:
# 1. Get age-specific rates from a reference (standard) population
# 2. Apply those rates to each study's age distribution to get "expected" cases
# 3. Compare observed cases to expected cases (Standardized Incidence Ratio, SIR)
# 4. SIR > 1 means more cases than expected; SIR < 1 means fewer cases than expected
std_ref <- read_dta(file.path(data_dir, "stdize_ind.dta"))

indirect_standardized <- collapsed |>
  left_join(std_ref, by = c("age_c4_code" = "age_c4")) |>
  group_by(study) |>
  summarise(
    observed = sum(ill, na.rm = TRUE),
    expected = sum(n * risk, na.rm = TRUE),
    sir = observed / expected,
    standardized_rate = sir * 0.15,
    .groups = "drop"
  )

Interpreting Standardized Results

Direct Standardization Results: - The crude_rate is the unadjusted rate (what you’d get if you just divided total cases by total people) - The std_rate is the age-standardized rate (adjusted for age differences) - Compare std_rate values between studies - these are now fair comparisons!

Indirect Standardization Results: - observed: How many cases actually occurred - expected: How many cases we’d expect based on the standard population - sir (Standardized Incidence Ratio): observed / expected - SIR = 1.0: Same as expected - SIR > 1.0: More cases than expected (e.g., SIR = 1.5 means 50% more cases) - SIR < 1.0: Fewer cases than expected (e.g., SIR = 0.8 means 20% fewer cases) - standardized_rate: The SIR multiplied by the reference rate

Key takeaway: Standardization lets you compare groups fairly by removing the effect of age (or other factors) that might make comparisons misleading (Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024).

5.6 Summary

This chapter has covered two fundamental concepts in epidemiological analysis:

5.6.1 Confidence Intervals

Confidence intervals provide a range of uncertainty around our estimates (Kirkwood & Sterne, 2003). They tell us not just “what we found” but “how certain we are about it.” Key points: - Approximate intervals: Work well with large samples, use normal distribution - Exact intervals: More precise, work even with small samples, use binomial distribution - Poisson intervals: For rates (events per person-time), account for counting events over time

5.6.2 Standardization

Standardization allows fair comparisons between groups with different characteristics (Dohoo, Martin, & Stryhn, 2012; Rothman, Huybrechts, & Murray, 2024): - Direct standardization: Apply age-specific rates to a standard population - more precise, requires detailed data - Indirect standardization: Compare observed to expected cases - simpler, useful when detailed data unavailable

5.6.3 Practical Applications

These methods are used throughout epidemiological research: - Reporting disease rates with confidence intervals (Batra et al., 2021) - Comparing rates between populations or time periods - Adjusting for confounding factors in descriptive analyses - Preparing data for more advanced analyses

For more detailed examples and R code, see the Epidemiologist R Handbook (Batra et al., 2021) and R4SME resources (Mazzella, n.d.).
